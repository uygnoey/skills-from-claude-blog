# An operating model for human-agent teams

Derived from the August 19, 2026 interview with Jaime DeLanghe, Chief Product
Officer at Slack — the second post in a series on building human-agent teams.

## The premise

The conversation around the work is the context agents need to be useful. For
years the hope was that the by-product of people working together would compound
into organizational knowledge; DeLanghe notes that early research at Slack found
it largely did not, and people went on repeating themselves. The volume was
never something humans could digest. That is now an agent's job — but only for
the part it can see.

Everything in this operating model follows from that single dependency.

## 1. Context: make it visible

- **Default to public channels.** Decisions made in DMs or private threads are
  invisible to agents and lost to the organization.
- **Ask for reasoning, not the record.** Instead of "what did we decide," ask an
  agent to reconstruct why, and how the context has shifted since.
- **Widen the surface area.** Connect meetings, email, calendar, and document
  repositories. More connected context means less repetition.

## 2. Workflow: a handoff cycle, not an automation

Agents handle production work — drafting, summarizing, monitoring, preparing —
and pass results to a person. The person reviews, decides, and redirects, then
hands work back for the next step.

- Start the day with agent-built briefings: recaps, escalations, meeting prep,
  web roundups.
- Anchor the loop in a shared channel where humans and agents triage together
  and humans lead prioritization.
- Make lightweight signals actionable — an emoji reaction that adds an item to
  the list and cues an agent to pick it up.

Every item closes with a human decision. That is the difference between a team
and a pipeline.

## 3. Roles: agents as coworkers

A fleet of specialized agents is disorienting to people whose mental model is a
single chatbot. Give each one goals and a focus area, the way a teammate has
responsibilities.

- Route routine, transactional tasks — filing a ticket, pulling last week's
  metrics into a status update — to a general agent, rather than asking people
  to remember a specialized tool for each.
- If people cannot articulate what an agent is for, retire it. Value that feels
  mandated rather than felt is a signal, not a training gap.

## 4. Openness: public by default, private on purpose

Open context compounds — new people onboard into history instead of an empty
inbox, and now agents benefit too.

- Keep non-sensitive projects, announcements, and Q&A in the open.
- Remember that agents read what your team reads: a private channel is a blind
  spot for every agent reporting on it.
- Once genuinely sensitive material is walled off, what pushes work back into
  DMs is usually discomfort with being seen mid-process. Psychological safety,
  not policy, is the lever. In DeLanghe's phrasing, "you gain trust by giving
  trust."

## 5. Adoption: demonstration over mandate

The fastest way to learn a new way of working is to watch a teammate do it.

- Stand up a company-wide, public-by-default show-and-tell channel where people
  share skills, debugging tips, and workflow tricks. A technique from one
  function can reshape another.
- Encourage short "what I did and how" write-ups. Inside Slack, a push to get
  product managers using Claude was largely self-organized this way: one PM
  documented his setup, others copied the format, and teams went on to run
  workshops and build their own repos.

## 6. Measurement: outcomes over activity

More messages never proved Slack was working — it can equally mean people
cannot find what they need. AI measurement has the same problem.

- Treat usage metrics as a pulse check, not proof of value. Token usage tells
  you the lights are on; that matters and is not sufficient.
- Be ready to use judgment. There is no clean way to prove that how people use
  these tools leads to better business results, and no dashboard will supply the
  missing link.

## How to start

Reimagine the workflow rather than doing the same work faster, and treat that as
a team sport. Start soon, but start small: bring a group of people into a shared
channel with an agent, give them the same set of resources, and let them work.
What they build tends to spread on its own.
